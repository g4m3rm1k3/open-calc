# Value: Respect

Respect is the value Kent Beck added later — the first edition of *Extreme
Programming Explained* named four values; the second added this one, explicitly,
because the author concluded the other four don't actually work without it. It's
worth understanding why a value about how people treat each other belongs in a book
about software engineering practices at all, rather than in a separate book about
management.

## Why Respect isn't a soft add-on to the other four

Consider what Communication (Post 2) actually requires: a junior developer has to
be willing to ask a question that might reveal they don't understand something,
and a senior developer has to be willing to hear "I think this design is wrong"
from someone with less experience without dismissing it by seniority alone. Neither
of those things happens on a team where people don't respect each other's
competence and time. Consider Courage (Post 5): admitting your own code needs to
change, or telling a customer a deadline is unrealistic, both require trusting that
honesty won't be punished — which is a direct function of whether the people
around you show real respect, or merely tolerate you as long as you don't cause
friction. Respect isn't a nice-to-have layered on top of the other values. It's the
condition that makes the other four actually safe to practice honestly instead of
performing.

## What this looks like, concretely, in engineering practice

Respect shows up as: not interrupting someone mid-thought during a pairing session
to grab the keyboard; taking a code review comment as being about the code, not an
attack on the person who wrote it, and phrasing your own review comments the same
way; crediting a good idea to whoever actually had it, including a junior teammate,
instead of letting it quietly become "leadership's idea" by the time it's adopted;
and — this is the one Beck returns to most directly — trusting that every person on
the team is doing their best with the understanding and constraints they actually
have, and treating a mistake as information rather than a character indictment.
None of this is abstract kindness for its own sake. Each one has a direct, causal
effect on whether the team's other values actually function or just get performed
in meetings.

## The specific failure mode this value prevents

A team without Respect can still go through the motions of every other practice in
this series — they can pair-program while one person silently disengages because
their input isn't valued; they can hold a retrospective where nobody says what
they actually think because the last person who did got quietly punished for it;
they can write tests while the person who wrote the most tests uses that as
leverage to belittle whoever wrote fewer. Every practice in Extreme Programming can
be technically followed and functionally hollowed out by a lack of Respect, which
is exactly why Beck eventually concluded it needed to be named directly rather than
assumed. A value that's implicit and unstated is a value a team can violate without
ever noticing they're doing it.

## Respect and psychological safety, named plainly

The modern research term for what this value is protecting is **psychological
safety** — the shared belief that the team is safe for interpersonal risk-taking,
that no one will be punished or humiliated for admitting a mistake, asking a
question, or offering an idea that turns out to be wrong. Teams with genuine
psychological safety measurably ship better software, not because being nice is
its own reward, but because every other value in this series — honest
Communication, the Courage to say "this is wrong" out loud, Feedback that people
actually act on instead of getting defensive about — requires it as a
precondition. Respect is the value that makes the other four load-bearing instead
of decorative.

## Where this shows up in practice

Pair Programming (Post 10) is the practice most directly dependent on Respect
being real rather than nominal — two people sharing a keyboard, constantly, is
either a genuinely collaborative practice or a genuinely miserable one, and the
entire difference is whether real respect is present. Whole-team practices —
planning together, retrospectives, code ownership shared rather than territorial —
are, in the end, all downstream of whether the team actually respects each other or
is merely co-located (or co-present on a call) without it. This is the last of the
five values in this series specifically because it's the one that determines
whether the other four are real.
