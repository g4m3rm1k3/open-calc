## Concept Unit: The Anatomy of a Good System

### The Problem
Many people say “I’ll exercise more” or “I’ll read every day,” but the intention is too vague. Without structure, it falls apart. We need a basic template that makes the system clear and executable.

### Introduce the concept in isolation
**Throwaway lab** (write this down):

**Bad system**: “I will study more.”  
**Better system**:  
- Trigger: After breakfast  
- Action: Study for 25 minutes using a specific lesson  
- Tracking: Mark ✓ in a notebook  
- Review: Every Sunday evening check how many days I did it

Try running the better version for 2 days.

**What this proves**: A good system has four clear parts: Trigger, Action, Tracking, and Review. This makes it much easier to execute consistently.

**Discard the throwaway example.**

### The Updated Project
Your workbook now has a clear 4-part system template.

### Mechanical walkthrough
1. **Trigger** — The cue that starts the behavior (e.g., time of day, after another habit, location). Good triggers are reliable and automatic.
2. **Action** — The specific behavior you will perform. It should be small and unambiguous.
3. **Tracking** — A simple way to record whether you did the action. This creates visibility and accountability.
4. **Review** — A regular moment to look at your tracking and adjust the system. This is the feedback loop.

### Systems Thinking Lens
This is a basic **feedback loop** design: Input (action) → Output (result) → Observation (review) → Adjustment.  
Also recognized in: thermostats, quality control in manufacturing, biological adaptation, and agile project management.

### Engineering / Design Lens
We chose a minimal 4-part template because it’s simple enough to start immediately but powerful enough to iterate on.  
**Alternative not chosen**: Making the system too complex from the beginning.  
**Tradeoff**: Simple systems are easier to start and maintain, but they may need refinement later. Starting overly complicated often leads to abandonment.
