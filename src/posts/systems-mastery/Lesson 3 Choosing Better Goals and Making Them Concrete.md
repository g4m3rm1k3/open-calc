## Concept Unit: Goal Quality Filters

### The Problem
Not all goals are equal. Many people chase goals that are vague, unimportant to them, or impossible to measure — then feel bad when they fail. We need filters to choose goals that are actually worth building systems for.

### Introduce the concept in isolation
**Throwaway lab** (write these down):

**Poor goal**: “Get better at life.”  
**Better goal**: “Be able to hold a 5-minute conversation in Spanish with a native speaker.”

Run both versions mentally for 30 seconds. Notice how the second one feels clearer and more motivating.

**What this proves**: Specific, personal, and measurable goals are much easier to turn into systems.

**Discard the throwaway example.**

### Project Change
- **Files affected**: Continue in `systems-mastery/lesson3.md`

### The New Code — type it yourself
Add to your workbook:

```markdown
# Systems Mastery - Lesson 3

## Current Goal
(From previous lessons)

## Apply the Filters
- Is it personally meaningful? (Why?)
- Is it specific enough? 
- Is it measurable? 
- Is it realistic in the next 3-6 months?

## Refined Goal (make it better)
(Write the improved version here)
```

### The Updated Project
Your workbook now includes a goal evaluation template.

### Mechanical walkthrough
1. **Personally meaningful?** — Does this goal actually matter to *you*, or are you chasing it because of external pressure?
2. **Specific enough?** — Can you clearly picture what success looks like?
3. **Measurable?** — How will you know when you’ve achieved it or made progress?
4. **Realistic?** — Is it achievable within a reasonable timeframe with your current resources?

### Systems Thinking Lens
This is **goal selection as a filtering process**. Good systems start with good inputs. Poor goals waste energy and create frustration loops.  
Also recognized in: engineering requirements gathering, business strategy (OKRs), and decision-making frameworks.

### Engineering / Design Lens
We chose these four filters because they are simple yet powerful.  
**Alternative not chosen**: Accepting any goal that “sounds good.”  
**Tradeoff**: Spending time filtering goals feels slower at first, but prevents building systems for the wrong things — which is one of the biggest hidden wastes of time and motivation.

### Run it
Take the goal from Lesson 2 and run it through the four filters. Then write a refined, better version.

### Connecting sentence
We now have better goals — next we will make them even more actionable with a powerful technique.

## Concept Unit: Making Goals Concrete with “Success Criteria”

### The Problem
Even after filtering, many goals are still too vague (“get fit”, “advance my career”). We need a way to define success so clearly that we know exactly what we’re aiming for.

### The New Code — type it yourself
Add to `lesson3.md`:

```markdown
## Success Criteria (How I will know I succeeded)
- Specific evidence or milestone:
- By when:
- What it looks like:
```

### The Updated Project
Your workbook now includes a concrete success definition.

### Mechanical walkthrough
1. **Specific evidence** — What exact thing will exist when you succeed?
2. **By when** — Adds a time boundary (helps with urgency and planning).
3. **What it looks like** — Describes the visible outcome in concrete terms.

### Systems Thinking Lens
This creates a clear **target state** for your feedback loop. Without it, you can’t tell if your system is working.

### Engineering / Design Lens
Defining success criteria upfront is like writing requirements before building a product. It prevents scope creep and makes progress measurable.  
**Tradeoff**: It can feel limiting at first (you might discover your original goal was too broad), but it dramatically increases the chance of real achievement.

### Run it
Refine your goal further by writing clear success criteria.
